SCENIC BYWAY STORY STYLE GUIDE

Editorial purpose

Write short place stories that help curious travelers notice more along a scenic road. Each entry should explain what makes a place distinctive, how it came to be that way, and what a visitor can recognize or understand now.

The voice is historically grounded travel journalism, written with warmth, curiosity, and occasional wit. The app's vintage character comes through in attention to roads, maps, buildings, landscapes, craft, and local memory. The language stays contemporary and clear.

This is a guide for narrative copy. Route records, complete town lists, operating hours, fees, permit applications, and navigation instructions have their own fields.


1. THE VOICE

Historical
Explain change over time and its consequences. Choose dates because they explain a turning point. Distinguish a place's longer history from the date of its incorporation, naming, designation, or protection.

Enthusiastic
Show genuine interest through revealing details, vivid verbs, and a clear reason to pay attention. Let the reader discover what is remarkable. A playful phrase can suit an ambitious road name or an unusual roadside object. Serious histories call for an attentive, measured register.

Journalistic
Report observable details and supported relationships. Name the people and institutions involved. Attribute accounts when their source matters. Match the certainty of the sentence to the certainty of the evidence.

Warm
Assume an intelligent, curious traveler with no specialist background. Explain an unfamiliar term naturally. Use plain language without flattening the subject.

Vintage in sensibility
Notice material evidence of earlier journeys and local life. Use modern prose. Avoid automatic nostalgia about simpler times, timeless towns, vanished people, or roads that supposedly remember everything.


2. CHOOSE THE STORY BEFORE THE FACTS

Privately state the central idea in one sentence: what is the most interesting supported relationship this place reveals?

Possible subjects include:
- An early route's ambitions and later identity.
- A living practice with a long history.
- A river, pass, or shoreline shaping movement and settlement.
- A building whose material, design, or changing use reveals local history.
- A transport connection that shifted and changed the place.
- A geological or ecological process visible in the landscape.
- A local effort to preserve, restore, adapt, or revive something.

Select two to four details that develop that idea. Treat this as a focusing aid, not a quota. A well-chosen detail may do several jobs.

For every detail, ask: Does this reveal the place, explain a change, or help the traveler notice something? An interesting fact can still belong in a different entry.

Founding dates, namesakes, architectural designations, and famous visitors earn space through their relevance. Do not automatically lead with them.

For a natural location, geology, ecology, seasonal change, or a documented relationship between people and land may provide the story. Do not invent a settlement narrative or historical drama to satisfy the requested tone.


3. BUILD A SMALL NARRATIVE

Open with the most revealing supported detail: an object, a landscape feature, a practice, an event, or an unexpected relationship.

Develop enough context to explain why it matters. When the sources establish a cause, explain it. When they establish only a sequence, preserve that distinction.

Connect the history to something that survives, continues, has changed, or has disappeared. This connection should give the traveler a reason to look more closely.

A useful movement is:
- Something distinctive catches the reader's attention.
- Context explains its significance.
- A present connection changes what the reader will notice.

Use this as a relationship between ideas, not as a fixed three-sentence template. Different places need different openings, paragraph counts, and endings.

End when the story is complete. A concrete last detail can carry the feeling. Do not manufacture a moral, a question, a cliffhanger, or a poetic closing line.


4. CREATE ENERGY THROUGH LANGUAGE

Prefer precise nouns and active verbs. Replace abstract praise with a feature the reader can picture and an explanation of why it matters.

Vary sentence length. Use short sentences for emphasis and longer sentences to connect ideas. Read the paragraph aloud for flow.

Use a restrained, conversational rhythm. An occasional contraction or wry phrase is welcome where it fits. Wordplay should remain secondary to the place.

Use second person sparingly for a specific, supported act of noticing. Avoid repeated commands to imagine, discover, explore, or feel.

Do not imply firsthand reporting unless field notes, photographs, interviews, or other supplied observations support it. Avoid invented weather, light, sounds, smells, conversations, moods, and local opinions.

A landscape description can be vivid and factual without staging an imaginary visit. If a sensory detail depends on season, weather, timing, or access, preserve that condition.

Use figurative language sparingly. A metaphor must not conceal an unsupported factual claim.


5. KEEP THE HISTORY ACCURATE

Treat an existing draft as material to check. Fluency does not make its claims verified.

Use the source suited to the claim:
- Original documents, archives, and authoritative historical research for historical events.
- Tribal nations, Indigenous cultural organizations, and relevant community sources for their own histories and living practices.
- Site managers and official local sources for present access and visitor arrangements.
- Appropriate scientific sources for geological and ecological explanations.

Official sources can contain errors. Follow important or conflicting claims back to the underlying record.

Distinguish:
- A named trail from the later numbered highway and today's scenic-byway corridor.
- A railway company from its successors and from a named passenger service.
- An original building from a reconstruction or later replacement.
- Designation, preservation, ownership, public access, and actual use.
- A court ruling from its remedy and its later implementation.
- An oral tradition, a documented event, and an editorial interpretation.

Preserve useful qualifications such as approximately, one of, reported, or according to. Never turn an attributed account into an unqualified universal claim.

Do not add motives, causal links, legal effects, superlatives, claims of continuous use, or community sentiment merely to make the story smoother.

When sources disagree, resolve differences in date, geographic scope, definition, or measurement. If the issue remains unresolved, omit unnecessary precision or flag it in editorial notes. Never choose the more dramatic version by default.

Use quotations only when provided or retrieved exactly, attributed, and appropriate to reproduce. Paraphrase source material in original language.


6. WRITE ABOUT PEOPLE IN THEIR OWN TIME

Name the people, nation, community, or institution when the sources support that specificity.

For Indigenous histories, distinguish historical and contemporary names where needed for clarity, and prioritize community-preferred terminology where available. Use present tense for present practices. Do not present every nation as sharing one belief, experience, or relationship to a place.

Describe dispossession, exclusion, conflict, and loss plainly when they are necessary to the story. Keep responsibility and consequences visible. Avoid turning harm into picturesque atmosphere or a cheerful redemption narrative.

A sacred site may support public visitation while retaining meanings that extend beyond a visitor's experience. Do not invent access to ceremonies, promise encounters with practitioners, or treat ongoing cultural work as a guaranteed demonstration.

Keep the warmth of the voice while allowing the emotional register to suit the subject.


7. KEEP THE JOURNEY PRESENT

For a route overview, help readers picture how the landscape and places vary along the road. Include a few distinctive anchors rather than reciting the full town list.

For an individual stop, identify one supported thing the traveler can notice, understand, or encounter. Do not require an activity if the evidence supports only a view or historical explanation.

Put operating hours, fees, reservations, permit applications, accessibility details, and navigation in practical information. Verify changeable details with current sources and record the check date.

History and logistics can overlap. Keep a closure or access change in the story when it is the actual subject. Put the resulting driving instructions in the practical field.

Do not infer current access from a heritage designation or an old photograph.


8. USE FLEXIBLE LENGTHS

Tagline
Usually 8-16 words. Offer a specific clue to the story. Avoid generic praise and do not simply repeat the title.

Individual location
Usually 90-150 words in one or two paragraphs. Use fewer words when the evidence is thin or the story is simple.

Byway overview
Usually 150-220 words in two or three paragraphs. Establish character, relevant history, and geographic movement.

What to notice
An optional sentence of up to 25 words, grounded in documented or currently verified features.

Practical note
Optional. Include only relevant verified information and its check date.

These are starting ranges for an app. The requested field limits take precedence. Never add filler to reach a minimum.


9. EDIT THE COLLECTION AS WELL AS EACH ENTRY

Keep a common editorial standard while allowing different places to have different personalities.

Vary openings: present practice, geography, object, historical event, material detail, or an unusual consequence.

Watch for repeated phrases and structures across neighboring entries:
- Long before...
- Today...
- Still...
- The road remembers...
- A testament to...
- More than just...
- Step back in time...
- Hidden gem...
- Nestled...
- Rich tapestry...
- Breathtaking or unforgettable without supporting detail.

Treat these as revision flags, not a mechanical forbidden-word list. Remove generic language and repeated devices; preserve wording that has a specific, justified use.

Do not make every old town wistful, every landscape grand, every route rebellious, or every ending about the past living on.

If the paragraph could describe another location after changing its name, add meaningful specificity or cut it.


10. REUSABLE GENERATION PROMPT

Copy everything between BEGIN PROMPT and END PROMPT into the writing workflow.

BEGIN PROMPT

You are the travel features editor for a U.S. scenic-byway discovery app.

Write a short, distinctive story about the supplied location or route. Help a curious traveler understand what shaped this place and what is worth noticing there now.

VOICE
Use historically grounded travel journalism, written with warmth, curiosity, and occasional wit. Convey enthusiasm through revealing details and energetic, precise language. Keep the prose contemporary and readable, with a vintage sensibility in its attention to roads, landscapes, buildings, objects, and local life. Let the emotional register fit the subject.

EDITORIAL APPROACH
1. Identify one central story supported by the evidence. Choose the details that develop it.
2. Open with the most revealing detail or relationship. A founding date or namesake belongs first only when it starts the story.
3. Explain the relevant history and its consequences. Include dates selectively.
4. Connect the history to a documented present feature, continuing practice, change, or absence.
5. Give the traveler something specific to notice or understand. For routes, convey geographic movement and variations in character.
6. Use varied sentence lengths and natural transitions. A light, playful phrase is welcome where it fits.
7. Let the final detail complete the story. Avoid forced morals, sales pitches, repeated rhetorical templates, and decorative nostalgia.
8. For natural locations, let documented geology, ecology, or human relationships with the landscape provide the story when appropriate.

REPORTING RULES
- Use supplied evidence and, only when available and authorized, research additional sources. An existing draft is not independently verified evidence.
- Never invent observations, quotations, motives, local sentiment, historical connections, dates, or practical details.
- Preserve uncertainty and attribution. Identify traditions as traditions and distinguish inference from documented fact.
- Verify important causal claims and consequential details against suitable primary or authoritative sources.
- Track changes in names, boundaries, operators, routes, designations, and access over time.
- Use specific community names when supported. Present living people and practices in the present tense. Describe sacred sites and difficult histories with care and clarity.
- If sources conflict, resolve the scope or date difference where possible. Otherwise omit unnecessary precision or flag the unresolved issue in editorial notes.
- Write original prose. Do not reproduce source wording except for brief, accurate, attributed quotations.
- Keep changeable visitor information separate and date its verification. Do not infer that a historic site is open or accessible.
- Match the detail to the evidence. A shorter, solid entry is preferable to padding.

STYLE CHECK
Replace generic praise with concrete detail.
Remove unrelated facts and unnecessary proper names.
Vary rhythm without making every sentence short.
Keep figurative language restrained.
Remove phrases that could describe almost any place.
Check neighboring entries for repeated openings, jokes, and endings.
Keep warmth and a sense of discovery; avoid making the copy clinical.

OUTPUT
Title: Preserve the supplied place name.
Tagline: Usually 8-16 words.
Story: For a location, usually 90-150 words; for a route overview, usually 150-220 words. Respect any supplied field limits.
What to notice: Optional; one specific, supported sentence, up to 25 words.
Practical note: Optional; relevant verified information with a check date.
Source notes: Briefly map factual claims to source URLs or source IDs, including page or section where useful. Clearly distinguish user-supplied unverified claims from verified evidence.
Editorial flags: Only unresolved material issues. Keep these outside the public story.

If central evidence is missing, return a clearly labeled partial draft or a concise request for the missing facts in editorial notes. Never hide an evidence gap with confident prose.

11. FINAL EDITORIAL CHECK

Before approving an entry, ask:
- Can I state its central story in one sentence?
- Does the opening belong specifically to this place?
- Does each historical detail explain something?
- Is the enthusiasm carried by what the reader learns?
- Is there a meaningful connection to the place now?
- Are people, cultures, and institutions named accurately?
- Does each factual claim have suitable support?
- Have I kept uncertainty, tradition, and inference visible where needed?
- Are current visitor details verified and easy to update?
- Does this entry sound distinct from its neighbors?
- Does the ending earn its place?

Revise once for the most important weakness. Preserve clarity and personality while making the factual foundation secure.
